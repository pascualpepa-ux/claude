# Reseki: market data on restaurant review responses + legal framework for B2B outreach in Spain

Research date: 2026-11-03. This is information, not legal advice. Many market figures come from vendor blogs; reliability is flagged item by item.

## A1. What share of restaurants/local businesses respond to Google reviews, and how fast?

### Takeaway
Restaurants answer a minority of their reviews. The best restaurant-specific estimate is about 28.5% of reviews across platforms (Sep 2025 to Sep 2026, mainly US data). Consumer expectations are high and rising: 89% expect replies, and 32% expect one by the next day. No Spain-specific response-rate study was found.

### Cited Findings
- Restaurants replied to only 28.5% of 90,471 reviews across Google, OpenTable, Yelp, TripAdvisor, Tock and Facebook (Sep 2025 to Sep 2026). By rating: 23.2% of 3-star reviews, 26.0% of 4-star reviews and 30.2% of 1-star reviews got a reply. This is a vendor analysis. — [Bloom Intelligence](https://bloomintelligence.com/blog/should-restaurants-respond-to-every-review/)
- Independent restaurants reply to about 15% of reviews and chains to more than half. This is a vendor claim and the underlying data was not verifiable. — [Wimi](https://getwimi.ai/blog/independent-restaurant-review-reply-rate)
- Small businesses in general (not only restaurants): 29.3% of 5-star Google reviews and 9.8% of 1-star reviews got a public reply, based on 229,882 reviews. — [UENI](https://ueni.com/blog/google-review-response-study/)
- In a 2022 LMBR figure cited by SOCi, the average business responded to 36.2% of its reviews. SOCi's own dataset showed lower rates. — [SOCi, State of Google Reviews](https://www.soci.ai/insights/state-of-google-reviews/)
- BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey 2026 (published 11 Feb 2026; 1,002 US adults):
  - 89% of consumers expect business owners to respond to reviews.
  - 80% are likely to use a business that responds to all reviews, and 42% are unlikely to use one that never responds.
  - Only 45% are likely to use a business that answers only positive reviews, and 47% one that answers only negative reviews.
  - 50% are unlikely to choose a business that uses templated or generic replies.
  - Expected response speed: 19% expect a same-day reply (6% in 2025), 32% by the next day (18% in 2025) and 81% within a week.
  - An owner's response ranks 5th among review factors (37%).
  - Source: [BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/consumer-review-survey/)

### Inferences
- With roughly 70 to 85% of restaurant reviews unanswered and nearly 9 in 10 consumers expecting replies, the gap that Reseki targets is real.
- The pitch should promise personalized replies, not templates, because 50% of consumers penalize generic answers. That is relevant for an AI product.
- Consumer expectations of fast replies are rising quickly year on year, which supports automation.

### Gaps
- No Spain or EU dataset on restaurant response rates or response times was found. All the figures above are US or global.
- No reliable data was found on how fast businesses actually respond (as opposed to what consumers expect).
- The BrightLocal page does not report what share of businesses respond.

## A2. Evidence that responding improves ratings, local SEO or bookings

### Takeaway
The strongest peer-reviewed evidence comes from Proserpio & Zervas (Marketing Science, 2017), using TripAdvisor hotels: after hotels started responding, they received 12% more reviews and ratings rose by about +0.12 stars. Google recommends responding but does not say replies are a ranking factor; review count and score do feed "prominence". Revenue claims (Womply's "+35%") are secondary and unverified.

### Cited Findings
- Proserpio & Zervas, "Online Reputation Management: Estimating the Impact of Management Responses on Consumer Reviews" (Marketing Science, 2017):
  - When hotels started responding, they received 12% more reviews and ratings rose 0.12 stars on average.
  - The mechanism: dissatisfied customers became less likely to post baseless negative reviews.
  - Control: ratings rose only on TripAdvisor (where the hotels responded), not on Expedia.
  - Trade-off: negative reviews became fewer but longer.
  - Sources: [INFORMS press release](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Want-to-improve-your-business-s-online-ratings-Make-sure-to-respond-to-reviews); [WARC](https://www.warc.com/newsandopinion/news/brands-get-better-ratings-by-replying-to-reviews/40051)
- Google's help page tells businesses to "Respond to reviews that users leave about your business… shows that you value your customers". Local ranking is based on relevance, distance and prominence. Review count and score factor into local ranking. — [Google Business Profile Help, "How to improve your local ranking"](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en)
- The claim that businesses responding consistently earn ~35% more revenue (Womply, 2019) is cited only secondhand in marketing blogs. — [Broadly blog](https://broadly.com/?p=13460) (secondary; original not verified)

### Inferences
- The defensible pitch is: (1) responding is Google's own recommendation; (2) peer-reviewed evidence links responding to more reviews and slightly higher ratings, which in turn affect Google prominence. "Replies directly boost ranking" should not be claimed as fact.
- The +0.12-star effect matters most where the displayed rating rounds near a threshold. That is an inference, and the study was on hotels, not restaurants.

### Gaps
- The original HBR article and the Cornell (Center for Hospitality Research) studies were not fetched.
- No restaurant-specific causal study on bookings was found.
- The Womply study was not verified at its original source.

## A3. Number of restaurants in Spain and Madrid (market sizing)

### Takeaway
Spain has about 84,000 to 87,000 restaurant premises (locales), plus about 164,000 bars. Figures differ by methodology. A Madrid-specific restaurant count was not found. The Comunidad de Madrid holds about 15.6% of all business premises in Spain.

### Cited Findings
- Hostelería de España: about 83,700 restaurant premises in January 2024 (+3.2% year on year), run by about 71,000 companies (+2.3%). — [Infobae/EP](https://www.infobae.com/espana/agencias/2024/12/10/la-restauracion-eleva-su-caja-un-71-hasta-septiembre-y-preve-un-2025-positivo/)
- Anuario de la Hostelería 2025 (figures for 2024):
  - Restaurants: 83,714 premises.
  - Bars: 163,890 (53.2% of the sector).
  - Total sector: 308,256 establishments. Another source gives 302,883.
  - Sources: [Diario de Gastronomía](https://diariodegastronomia.com/las-cifras-la-hosteleria-espana-2025-las-previsiones-2026/); [aetcm (280,400 figure, different scope)](https://aetcm.es/la-hosteleria-en-espana-en-2025-280-400-establecimientos-transformacion-y-consolidacion/); full PDF at [otea.es](https://otea.es/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/ANUARIO-HOSTELERIA-2025.pdf) (too large to fetch)
- INE DIRCE at 1 Jan 2025, as reported by a secondary source:
  - 73,296 companies and 86,968 premises in CNAE 561 (restaurants and food stalls).
  - 234,900 companies and 270,205 premises in food and beverage services overall.
  - Source: [ChefBusiness](https://chefbusiness.co/estadisticas-hosteleria-espana-2026/) / [elmetodo360](https://elmetodo360.com/hosteleria-en-cifras/). The original INE table was not verified.
- INE DIRCE 2025 press release:
  - The Comunidad de Madrid accounts for 15.6% of Spain's 3,866,958 business premises.
  - Madrid has 27,687 active companies in "Hostelería". This category includes accommodation and bars, so it is not the same as restaurants.
  - Source: [INE nota de prensa DIRCE 2025](https://www.ine.es/dyngs/Prensa/DIRCE2025.htm)
- Statista has a table of restaurants by autonomous community (INE, 1 Jan 2024), but the figures were not visible. — [Statista](https://es.statista.com/estadisticas/646140/restaurantes-y-puestos-de-comida-por-cc-aa-espana)

### Inferences
- Rough Madrid estimate, unverified: if Madrid holds about 13 to 16% of restaurant premises, that is roughly 11,000 to 14,000 restaurants in the Comunidad de Madrid. Confirm in the INE DIRCE regional table.
- Rough TAM, illustrative only: about 87k restaurants × 39 USD × 12 ≈ 40M USD/year. Including bars that rely on Google reviews would raise the ceiling.

### Gaps
- No verified count of restaurants in the Comunidad de Madrid or the city of Madrid.
- The INE CNAE 561 figure was not verified directly on ine.es.

## B1. LSSI-CE art. 21–22: does the cold-email ban cover businesses (legal persons)?

### Takeaway
Yes. The LSSI's definition of "destinatario" expressly includes legal persons, and art. 21.1 bans unsolicited promotional email to "destinatarios". B2B cold email to info@restaurante.es is therefore prohibited without prior request or express authorization, unless there is a prior contractual relationship (art. 21.2). Fines range up to €30,000 (minor) or €30,001 to €150,000 (serious, for mass sending). Actual AEPD fines are usually hundreds to a few thousand euros.

### Cited Findings
- Art. 21.1 LSSI: "Queda prohibido el envío de comunicaciones publicitarias o promocionales por correo electrónico u otro medio de comunicación electrónica equivalente que previamente no hubieran sido solicitadas o expresamente autorizadas por los destinatarios de las mismas." — [BOE consolidated LSSI](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2002-13758)
- Annex (d) defines "destinatario" as "persona física o jurídica que utiliza, sea o no por motivos profesionales, un servicio de la sociedad de la información". — [BOE](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2002-13758)
- Annex (f) defines "comunicación comercial" as "toda forma de comunicación dirigida a la promoción, directa o indirecta, de la imagen o de los bienes o servicios". — [BOE](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2002-13758)
- Art. 20.1: a commercial electronic communication must be clearly identifiable as such, and so must its sender. Art. 22.1: consent can be revoked at any time by simple notice. — [BOE](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2002-13758)
- Sanctions — [BOE](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2002-13758):
  - Art. 38.4.d (minor): sending commercial communications by email.
  - Art. 38.3.c (serious): mass sending.
  - Art. 39.1: minor up to €30,000; serious €30,001 to €150,000.
- Exception, art. 21.2: prior contractual relationship, similar products and an opt-out offered. — [coolabora.es](https://coolabora.es/las-comunicaciones-comerciales-a-traves-de-medios-electronicos/)
- Commentators state that the AEPD applies art. 21 to legal persons as well. Consent is required whether the recipient is a natural or legal person, and the LSSI prevails over the LOPDGDD for the email channel:
  - [Fieldfisher](https://www.fieldfisher.com/es-es/locations/spain/insights/bases-datos-empresariales) (page returned 403; claim from search summary)
  - [legitec.com](https://legitec.com/envios-comerciales-sin-consentimiento-la-aepd-ya-esta-sancionando-con-hasta-5-000-e/)
  - [enbuscadelfuego.com](https://enbuscadelfuego.com/cold-email-legal-espana/)
  - [clyvert.com](https://clyvert.com/como-cumplir-el-rgpd-al-hacer-email-marketing-a-clientes-b2b/)
- Dissenting view: one vendor blog says the AEPD accepts legitimate interest for B2B email to professional addresses. — [overloop.com](https://overloop.com/es/blog/b2b-cold-email-espana-rgpd-aepd). This contradicts the statutory text and the other sources. Treat it as unreliable; Overloop sells cold-email software.
- Sanction examples:
  - PS/00245/2022: €800 for emails sent without consent. — [AEPD](https://www.aepd.es/es/documento/reposicion-ps-00245-2022.pdf)
  - €5,000 fine on an energy consultancy for repeated unsolicited advertising. — [Economist & Jurist](https://www.economistjurist.es/actualidad-juridica/jurisprudencia/sancionada-con-5-000-euros-una-consultora-energetica-por-el-envio-constante-y-no-autorizado-de-publicidad-a-una-persona)
  - Other AEPD art. 21 resolutions, e.g. [E/02208/2018](https://www.aepd.es/documento/e-02208-2018.pdf).
  - Art. 39 bis allows warnings or corrective measures instead of fines in some cases. — [AEPD E/07828/2015](https://www.aepd.es/documento/e-07828-2015.pdf)
- AEPD legal report 2018-0164 discusses art. 21. — [AEPD](https://www.aepd.es/documento/2018-0164.pdf) (not fully read)

### Inferences
- Sending even a single unsolicited promotional email to a restaurant's info@ address is a minor infringement on the plain text of the law. Practical enforcement risk per complaint is probably low (hundreds to a few thousand euros), but it is non-zero and grows with volume ("envío masivo" makes it serious).
- An email that asks for permission to send information is arguably itself a commercial communication under the broad definition in Annex (f). This is a legal grey area, flagged as uncertain.

### Gaps
- No AEPD resolution was found that specifically sanctions B2B cold email to a generic business address. The examples found involve individuals.

## B2. RGPD/GDPR and LOPDGDD: generic vs named emails, legitimate interest, art. 14

### Takeaway
GDPR applies only to personal data. A purely generic address (info@) of a company is arguably not personal data, but a sole trader's address or juan@restaurante.es is. LOPDGDD art. 19 presumes it lawful to process the professional contact data of people working for a legal person. Even so, the LSSI still governs whether you may email them commercially.

### Cited Findings
- The AEPD's Circular 1/2023 (art. 5) restates LOPDGDD art. 19: "Se presumirá lícito el tratamiento de los datos de las personas físicas que presten servicios en una persona jurídica…". — [BOE, Circular 1/2023](https://www.boe.es/eli/es/cir/2023/06/26/1/con)
- Art. 19 LOPDGDD allows processing of professional data under legitimate interest, but that does not authorize commercial email, which is governed by the LSSI. — [enbuscadelfuego.com](https://enbuscadelfuego.com/cold-email-legal-espana/)

### Inferences
- Under GDPR art. 14, when data is not obtained from the data subject (e.g. scraped from Google Maps), the controller must give the privacy information within a reasonable period: at the latest at first communication, or within one month. This is general knowledge and was not re-verified this session. Check EUR-Lex Regulation 2016/679 art. 14.
- Many restaurants are sole traders (autónomos). Their business email or phone often is personal data, so GDPR fully applies.

### Gaps
- The GDPR text (art. 6.1.f, art. 14) was not fetched from EUR-Lex.
- No AEPD guidance specifically on info@ addresses was found.

## B3. Legally safer alternatives (phone, Lista Robinson, forms, visits, LinkedIn, post, consent)

### Takeaway
Commercial phone calls since June 2023 (Ley 11/2022 art. 66.1.b + AEPD Circular 1/2023) require prior consent or a documented legitimate-interest balancing test done in advance. Calls to random numbers need consent, numbers taken from directories need express consent, and exclusion systems (Lista Robinson) must always be checked first. Whether this covers calls to companies is ambiguous. In-person visits and postal mail are outside the LSSI, though postal mail to named persons is still GDPR processing.

### Cited Findings
- Art. 66.1.b LGTel: the right not to receive unsolicited commercial calls, except with prior consent or another GDPR art. 6.1 basis. — [AEPD info sheet](https://www.aepd.es//documento/info-derechos-llamadas-comerciales-no-solicitadas.pdf); [INAP](https://laadministracionaldia.inap.es/noticia.asp?id=1234774)
- Circular 1/2023 — [BOE](https://www.boe.es/eli/es/cir/2023/06/26/1/con):
  - In force from 29 June 2023.
  - Art. 3.1–3.2: legitimate interest is allowed only after a prior weighing test.
  - Art. 3.4: no presumption of reasonable expectation without a current contract or an interaction within the past year.
  - Art. 4: advertising exclusion systems must be consulted beforehand (LOPDGDD art. 23).
  - The text frames the right around "usuarios finales" and does not expressly address calls to companies.
- Random-number calls require consent; directory numbers require express consent; Robinson must be consulted. — [Economist & Jurist](https://www.economistjurist.es/actualidad-juridica/las-empresas-no-pueden-realizar-llamadas-comerciales-a-numeros-aleatorios); [Pérez-Llorca note](https://perezllorca.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Nota-Juridica-Nueva-normativa-que-prohibe-las-llamadas-comerciales-sin-autorizacion-1.pdf)

### Inferences
Ranked roughly from lowest to highest legal risk. This is an inference and needs a lawyer's confirmation.
1. In-person visits to restaurants (no LSSI or LGTel).
2. Inbound marketing: content, SEO, a free audit of unanswered reviews, partnerships with hospitality associations or POS vendors.
3. Postal mail to the business (outside LSSI; minimal GDPR exposure if addressed to the company).
4. LinkedIn connection or InMail (platform rules apply; arguably "electronic communication" — grey area).
5. Contact-form submissions (likely still "equivalent electronic communication" under art. 21 — grey area).
6. Phone calls with a documented legitimate-interest assessment and a Robinson check.
7. Cold email (clearly within the art. 21 prohibition).

### Gaps
- No authoritative source was found on whether a restaurant's switchboard number (a legal person) is protected under art. 66 LGTel.
- No source was found on contact forms or LinkedIn under the LSSI.

## B4. Google terms on scraping Maps / Places API for lead lists

### Takeaway
The Google Maps Platform Terms prohibit scraping and building datasets from Google Maps Content ("No Scraping", "No Creating Content From Google Maps Content", currently numbered around §3.2.3) and limit caching (Place IDs are an exception). Building a lead list of restaurants with emails and phones from Maps or the Places API likely breaches these terms.

### Cited Findings
- The terms include "No Scraping" (no extracting or exporting Maps Content for use outside the Services) and "No Creating Content From Google Maps Content" (including creating other datasets). Numbered §3.2.4 in the 2018 version and reported as §3.2.3(a)/(b) in 2026 versions. — [GitHub issue quoting the terms](https://github.com/m-wrzr/populartimes/issues/90); [Thunderbit](https://thunderbit.com/sv/blog/is-scraping-google-maps-legal); [Cesium restatement](https://cesium.com/legal/terms-for-google/)
- A user reports a 24-hour account suspension after scraping, citing §3.2.3. This is anecdotal. — [GitHub](https://github.com/m-wrzr/populartimes/issues/90)
- Official terms page: [cloud.google.com/maps-platform/terms](https://cloud.google.com/maps-platform/terms). The fetch was truncated, so the current wording was not verified.

### Inferences
- A compliant alternative is to use the Places API only in-product: the restaurant owner connects their own Google Business Profile via OAuth, which Reseki needs anyway to post replies. Prospects should come from public directories, events or associations rather than a scraped Maps database.

### Gaps
- The current official numbering and wording were not verified.
- Google Business Profile API access and approval requirements were not researched.
